HOA Board vs. Management Company in Arizona: Who Decides and Who Gets Paid

Most HOA notices arrive on management-company letterhead, but the decisions behind them belong to the association and its board. Knowing who does what, and what each side is paid or rewarded for, makes it easier to send your question to the right person. These answers cover Arizona planned communities; condo owners should see the note near the end.

Who is in charge

Who is actually in charge, the board or the management company?

The association is run by its board of directors, which owners elect. A management company is a vendor the board hires by contract to carry out day-to-day work such as inspections, letters, collections, and bookkeeping. The manager takes direction from the board, not the other way around.

Who decides whether I get fined?

Arizona's statute gives the power to impose penalties to the board (A.R.S. 33-1803(B)). In many communities the manager runs inspections and sends letters under a policy or fine schedule the board adopted. Ask in writing who approved your fine and under which policy, and request the related records and minutes (A.R.S. 33-1805).

Does the management company work for me?

No. It works for the association under contract and takes direction from the board. It can be a helpful channel for routine questions, but it isn't your representative, so put anything important in writing and copy the board.

Can I go directly to the board?

Yes. Write to the board (ask the manager to forward your letter, or use the board address in your documents), and speak at an open board meeting: members may speak once after the board discusses an agenda item and before it takes formal action (A.R.S. 33-1804(A)). See our board meetings guide.

Who do I complain to about the management company?

The board, because it hires, oversees, and can replace the manager. Put the complaint in writing with dates and examples, and ask that it be placed on a future agenda. An ADRE petition is brought against the association, not the manager, and covers violations of the community documents or HOA statutes (A.R.S. 32-2199.01(A)).

Who gets paid, and incentives

Are HOA board members paid?

In most communities, board members are volunteer owners who pay the same assessments you do and live under the same rules. Whether directors can be compensated depends on your governing documents, so check the bylaws.

How does the management company get paid?

Usually through a base management fee in its contract with the association. Many contracts also list extra charges, for example for collection work, resale paperwork, extra meetings, or certain letters. The terms vary by contract, and the contract is an association record you can ask to examine (A.R.S. 33-1805).

Does the management company make money from my fine?

Fines are generally owed to the association, not the manager. Whether the manager earns anything connected to enforcement, such as per-letter, per-inspection, or collection fees, depends on its contract. Don't assume either way; ask for the contract and fee schedule.

What incentives does each side have?

Board members are neighbors with a stake in property values, the budget, and their own re-election, and they often rely heavily on the manager's advice. A management company is a business that wants to keep the contract, satisfy the board that hired it, and handle many communities efficiently, which tends to favor form letters and consistent, by-the-book enforcement. If its contract pays for letters or collection work, more of that activity can mean more revenue. None of this means either side is acting in bad faith, but it explains why a calm, specific written question to the board often gets a different answer than a phone call to the manager.

Can I see the management contract and the HOA's financial records?

Yes. Financial and other association records, including contracts, must be made reasonably available for examination by members; review is free and the HOA has 10 business days (A.R.S. 33-1805(A)). See our records request guide.

Fines and the management company

Can fines keep adding up every day or week?

The statute doesn't directly address recurring fines; the same rules of reasonableness, notice, and an opportunity to be heard apply (A.R.S. 33-1803(B)). Ask in writing for the fine schedule or policy the board adopted and how each charge on your ledger was calculated.

My neighbors do the same thing. Can the HOA single me out?

Document it with dates and photos, and request records of how similar violations or requests were handled (A.R.S. 33-1805). Uneven enforcement is a fair question to raise with the board in writing or at an open board meeting.

How do I appeal a fine?

Ask the board in writing for the hearing you're entitled to under A.R.S. 33-1803(B). The board may hear a member's appeal of a violation or penalty in closed session unless that member asks for it to be open (A.R.S. 33-1804(A)). If it isn't resolved, see our ADRE petition guide.

Step by step: getting an answer from the right people

  1. Note the date on any notice and count 21 calendar days for a certified-mail response.
  2. Find out who decided it. Ask in writing which rule was violated, who observed it, and whether the board approved the penalty.
  3. Ask for your opportunity to be heard if the notice didn't offer one.
  4. Request records: your violation file, the fine schedule or policy, and the management contract if fees are involved.
  5. Take it to the board in writing or at an open meeting if the manager can't resolve it.

Sample wording

I am writing to the Board of Directors about the penalty dated [date] for [address]. Please tell me which provision was violated, who observed it, and whether the board approved this penalty under A.R.S. 33-1803(B). I request my opportunity to be heard. Under A.R.S. 33-1805, I also ask to examine the fine schedule or enforcement policy in effect on that date and my violation file.

If you live in a condo

Condo associations follow parallel rules: penalties only after notice and an opportunity to be heard (A.R.S. 33-1242(A)(11)), the same 21-day and 10-business-day exchange (33-1242(B)–(D)), records under 33-1258, and open meetings under 33-1248. Condo associations also have a statutory duty to exercise discretionary powers neutrally, fairly, without favoritism and in a nonarbitrary way (A.R.S. 33-1242(E)).
